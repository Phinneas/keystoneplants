The white oak is the keystone tree of the eastern United States. Plant one in a suburban yard in Virginia or Pennsylvania and you're potentially supporting over 400 caterpillar species, dozens of specialist bees, and hundreds of bird species that depend on those insects to raise their young.

But if you're gardening in California, a white oak isn't your keystone tree. In the Mediterranean California ecoregion, the keystone tree rankings look substantially different — and the species at the top of that list are ones most Californians have never considered planting.

This guide covers the keystone trees of the Mediterranean California ecoregion, with particular attention to one of the most ecologically valuable and underplanted: the California sycamore (*Platanus racemosa*).

---

## The Mediterranean California Ecoregion: A Different Keystone Hierarchy

The NWF's keystone plant data for Mediterranean California (Ecoregion 11) shows a distinct hierarchy from eastern ecoregions. The rankings are based on how many butterfly and moth caterpillar species each plant genus supports, combined with pollen specialist bee data.

The top keystone trees in California's Mediterranean ecoregion:

| Plant Genus | Common Name | Caterpillar Species |
|-------------|-------------|---------------------|
| *Salix* | Willow | 328 |
| *Quercus* | Oak | 275 |
| *Prunus* | Native cherry/plum | 262 |
| *Populus* | Cottonwood, aspen | 230 |
| *Pinus* | Native pines | 220 |
| *Alnus* | Alder | 202 |
| *Acer* | Maple | 120 |
| *Ceanothus* | California lilac | 120 |

Two things stand out immediately. First, native willows (*Salix*) top the list — supporting **328 caterpillar species** in California's ecoregion, more than any other tree genus. Second, *Ceanothus* (California lilac) appears at 120 caterpillar species — a genus almost entirely absent from eastern keystone lists. These are California-specific keystones that reflect the distinct evolutionary relationships that developed in this unique climate.

## California Sycamore (*Platanus racemosa*)

The California sycamore is one of the region's most distinctive native trees — and one of the most underappreciated as a keystone plant.

Found only in California and Baja California (generally below 4,000 feet elevation), *Platanus racemosa* is a riparian tree of streamsides, canyons, and valley bottoms. It doesn't appear in the top 30 caterpillar-support genera for the Mediterranean California ecoregion — but it plays an ecological role that the species count alone doesn't capture.

### What the Sycamore Provides

**Structural habitat at scale.** A mature California sycamore reaches 75–100 feet, with one or multiple massive trunks and an open, irregular crown. The City of Glendale's urban forestry data documents that oak, sycamore, and bay trees together provide habitat for **81 species of resident and migratory birds** in California's urban and suburban landscapes.

**Riparian habitat anchor.** California sycamore is a keystone of the riparian woodland ecosystem — the narrow, water-adjacent corridors that support disproportionate biodiversity in California's dry landscape. These corridors function as migration pathways, breeding habitat for warblers and flycatchers, and the only year-round habitat in regions with prolonged summer drought. Without sycamores in the riparian structure, the habitat structure collapses.

**Fast canopy establishment.** Unlike oaks, which grow slowly, California sycamore is a fast-growing tree — sometimes adding 15–20 feet in five years. For gardeners and restoration projects that need large-canopy wildlife habitat quickly, sycamore provides a canopy overhead while slower-growing oaks establish below.

**Nesting and cavity habitat.** As sycamores age, they develop hollow trunks and large branches — critical nesting sites for cavity-nesting birds including woodpeckers, western screech-owls, and wood ducks. Old sycamores in riparian corridors function as the housing infrastructure of the surrounding bird community.

### California Sycamore in the Garden

Despite growing to 100 feet, California sycamore can be managed as a multi-trunk specimen in larger suburban yards (minimum half-acre). Its shallow roots require moist, well-drained soil — it doesn't establish well in heavy clay or in sites far from seasonal water.

It's deciduous (an advantage for summer-drought landscapes — it drops leaves in fall, then regrows in spring), and the large, maple-like leaves create significant leaf litter that serves as overwintering habitat for ground-nesting insects.

Plant in fall for best establishment in California's climate — the rainy season from November through March does the establishment work so the tree can handle summer drought. Once established (typically 2–3 years), California sycamore is drought-tolerant and largely self-sustaining.

## The Top Keystone Tree Choice: Native Willows (*Salix* spp.)

The data is unambiguous in Mediterranean California: **native willows are the single highest-impact tree planting decision**, supporting 328 caterpillar species — more than coast live oak, more than native pines, more than any other tree genus in the ecoregion.

This ranking surprises many California gardeners, who tend to think of willows as weedy or messy. But native willows are also one of the earliest trees to bloom in spring — producing pollen-rich catkins when native bees first emerge from hibernation and little else is available. They're irreplaceable early-season resources.

**Native willows for California gardens:**

- **Arroyo willow (*S. lasiolepis*)** — the most common California riparian willow; adaptable to streamside or seasonally moist sites; dense growth provides bird nesting cover
- **Coyote willow (*S. exigua*)** — narrow-leafed, shrubby form; excellent for bank stabilization and small riparian areas; handles seasonal flooding
- **Yellow willow (*S. lutea*)** — Central Valley and mountain sites; rich yellow bark; supports diverse insects

For gardens without riparian access, shrubby willows in seasonally moist areas are manageable alternatives to large tree willows — same ecological value, smaller footprint.

## Coast Live Oak (*Quercus agrifolia*)

The signature oak of California's coast ranges, coast live oak is evergreen — unlike eastern oaks, it holds its leaves year-round, providing consistent structure and year-round insect habitat. The NWF data places oaks second in California's keystone rankings at 275 caterpillar species.

Over 270 species of birds and wildlife depend on coast live oaks for food and habitat. Acorns are a primary food source for acorn woodpeckers (which store thousands in excavated "granary trees"), band-tailed pigeons, mule deer, and black bears. The tree's complex bark and dense canopy support a community of insects that feeds resident and migratory birds year-round.

**Key species:**
- **Coast live oak (*Q. agrifolia*)** — coastal ranges; medium-large tree, 25–82 feet; evergreen; extremely drought-tolerant once established
- **Canyon live oak (*Q. chrysolepis*)** — foothills and mountain slopes; tough, drought-tolerant; evergreen; smaller stature than coast live oak in challenging sites
- **Valley oak (*Q. lobata*)** — California's largest oak; deciduous; Central Valley and foothills; extremely long-lived; highest individual wildlife value of any California oak

Valley oak deserves special mention: it's the largest native oak west of the Rockies, can live 600+ years, and produces the acorns most consumed by California's wildlife community. Where space allows, valley oak is the single highest-return long-term investment available to a California gardener or restoration project.

## California Lilac (*Ceanothus* spp.) — A California Keystone Original

*Ceanothus* appears nowhere on eastern keystone plant lists — it's a primarily California and western genus that evolved specific relationships with California's native insect community. With 120 caterpillar species supported in Mediterranean California, it ranks alongside maple as a keystone tree/shrub that has no equivalent in eastern gardening.

Beyond caterpillars, *Ceanothus* is a critical nitrogen-fixer — its root nodules host bacteria that convert atmospheric nitrogen into a form plants can use, improving soil fertility for surrounding plants. It also produces abundant blue, white, or purple flowers in spring that are among the most important nectar sources for native bees in California's Mediterranean climate.

**Key species:**
- **Chaparral whitethorn (*C. leucodermis*)** — mid-elevation chaparral; drought-tolerant; white flowers
- **Blue blossom (*C. thyrsiflorus*)** — coastal ranges; one of the tallest species, to 20 feet; stunning blue-purple flowers
- **Buckbrush (*C. cuneatus*)** — dry, rocky slopes; fire-adapted; often first to recolonize disturbed sites
- **Deerbrush (*C. integerrimus*)** — white to pale blue flowers; mountain slopes and mixed conifer forests; important deer and insect browse

The key limitation of *Ceanothus*: most species are fire-adapted shrubs or small trees with relatively short life spans (15–25 years) compared to oaks. Plan for replacement planting as older specimens decline.

## Native Alder (*Alnus rhombifolia*, White Alder)

White alder is the riparian counterpart to willow — a nitrogen-fixing native tree that supports 202 caterpillar species in the Mediterranean California ecoregion and anchors streamside habitat across much of the state.

It grows fast (comparable to sycamore), tolerates seasonal flooding, and produces catkins that are critical early spring pollen sources. Its root nodules fix nitrogen, enriching the streamside soils that benefit surrounding riparian plants.

For riparian restoration projects or streamside gardens, the natural plant community is: willow + alder + sycamore + native cottonwood, with understory shrubs including native dogwood, elderberry, and wild rose. This combination covers the full keystone tree layer for California's riparian ecosystems.

## Building a California Keystone Tree Guild

The most ecologically effective planting approach in California (or anywhere) is to establish a **keystone guild** — a combination of trees and shrubs that work together to cover seasonal food availability, vertical habitat structure, and multiple species' needs simultaneously.

A basic California keystone guild for a medium to large suburban lot:

**Overstory (1–2 trees depending on space):**
- Valley oak or coast live oak for long-term acorn production and caterpillar support
- California sycamore (if near seasonal water or drainage) for structural habitat and rapid canopy

**Mid-story (2–4 shrubs/small trees):**
- Native willow (arroyo willow or coyote willow for wet areas)
- *Ceanothus* (blue blossom or buckbrush for spring bloom)
- Toyon (*Heteromeles arbutifolia*) — a California endemic with 96 specialist bee species support, critical winter berries for birds
- Coffeeberry (*Frangula californica*) — 33 caterpillar species; berries consumed by birds and coyotes

**Ground layer:**
- Native buckwheats (*Eriogonum* spp.) — California-specific keystone perennials with 21+ specialist bee species
- *Phacelia* spp. — 32 specialist bee species in California; excellent spring bloomers
- Native sunflowers (*Helianthus*) — 41 specialist bee species in California ecoregion

This guild provides food resources from early spring (willow catkins, *Ceanothus* bloom) through fall (oak acorns, toyon berries), with year-round caterpillar host plants and winter fruit.

## For Gardeners Outside California

The keystone tree principle applies in every ecoregion — the specific species change, but the approach is consistent: identify the trees with the highest caterpillar support and specialist bee support in your ecoregion, then plant the one or two that fit your site conditions.

The NWF's keystone plant lists for each of the 12 ecoregions provide this data. For most ecoregions outside California, oaks (*Quercus*) and native cherries (*Prunus*) top the caterpillar rankings, with willows and birches close behind.
