## Supply shortages and production difficulty reshape what makes it onto shelves

Native plants can be harder to produce commercially than ornamentals. That's not a moral judgment, it's logistics. Many natives need more time to reach a size that looks "ready to sell," and that adds cost and uncertainty for growers.

> **Did You Know?** 52% of nursery professionals cite a lack of supply as their biggest challenge when selling native plants. *Source: Lawn Love.*

When supply is tight, garden centers make choices that reduce risk. They lean toward plants that are easier to scale and easier to sell in volume. That can inadvertently elevate invasives because some invasive plants are also fast to grow and widely available.

So if you're asking, "Why are invasives showing up next to the native signs?" The simplest answer is timing and feasibility. The real reason garden centers prioritize invasives over natives is often that they are managing what arrives, not what's ideal for the ecosystem.

## Labor and "fussy" growth turn into real purchasing math

In 2026, costs are not just about soil and pots. Labor matters. Many natives take years to reach sellable size, and "finicky" growth can mean more hands-on care, more monitoring, and more losses when a batch doesn't perform.

That changes the buying equation. A plant that costs less to raise and sells more quickly helps a business keep cash moving. In contrast, a native plant that needs more time and attention can be a slower investment.

To be clear, that's not an excuse. It's the reason the shelf ends up being a compromise. If we want more Native Plant options in our neighborhoods, we need better pathways that don't rely on garden centers having unlimited time to experiment.
